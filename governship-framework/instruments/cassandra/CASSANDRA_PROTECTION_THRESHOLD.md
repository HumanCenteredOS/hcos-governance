HCOS™ Cassandra Protection Threshold
Human-Centered Decision Thresholds for Protection Under Uncertainty
Framework: Human-Centered Operating Systems™ (HCOS™)
Instrument Family: HCOS™ Cassandra
Purpose
The HCOS™ Cassandra Protection Threshold helps determine what level of action is justified when evidence remains incomplete.
Its central principle is:
Protection and proof are different decisions.
A system should not require the same evidentiary threshold for listening, investigation, temporary protection, formal findings, and irreversible consequences.
The appropriate threshold depends on:
Evidence strength.
Potential severity of harm.
Vulnerability.
Urgency.
Power imbalance.
Reversibility.
Cost of waiting.
Cost of acting incorrectly.
1. Why Protection Thresholds Matter
Two harmful errors commonly occur.
Threshold Too High
The system requires near certainty before allowing protection.
Possible result:
Harm continues.
Vulnerable people remain exposed.
Evidence becomes harder to obtain.
Distress worsens.
The messenger learns that speaking does not lead to protection.
Threshold Too Low
A serious conclusion is reached from minimal evidence.
Possible result:
Unfair accusation.
Disproportionate restriction.
Reputation damage.
Relationship rupture.
Misidentification of the actual cause.
Human-centered governship requires different thresholds for different actions.
2. Protection Ladder
Level 1 — Listen
Threshold: A sincerely raised concern.
Appropriate actions:
Listen without immediate judgment.
Clarify what is being reported.
Identify immediate needs.
Separate experience from interpretation.
Listening does not imply agreement.
Level 2 — Clarify
Threshold: Relevant uncertainty exists.
Appropriate actions:
Ask neutral questions.
Identify direct observations.
Explore competing explanations.
Determine whether additional information exists.
Level 3 — Document
Threshold: Meaningful impact, repeated concern, or potential pattern.
Appropriate actions:
Preserve messages.
Record dates and observations.
Track changes in functioning.
Separate factual notes from interpretation.
Documentation should support understanding, not retaliation.
Level 4 — Investigate
Threshold: Reasonable concern supported by meaningful observations or risk.
Appropriate actions:
Seek additional evidence.
Consult legitimate independent sources.
Review records.
Look for corroboration and contradiction.
Level 5 — Temporary Protection
Threshold: Credible possibility of meaningful harm.
Temporary protection may include:
Reduced exposure.
Increased supervision.
Written communication.
Temporary reassignment.
Privacy restrictions.
Independent support.
Temporary alternative arrangements.
Temporary protection does not establish guilt.
Level 6 — Strong Protective Action
Threshold: Significant evidence, escalating risk, severe impact, or high vulnerability.
Possible actions:
Formal safeguarding procedures.
Professional intervention.
Significant contact restrictions.
Organizational escalation.
Legal consultation.
Independent investigation.
Level 7 — Emergency Action
Threshold: Immediate or credible danger of serious harm.
Action should prioritize immediate safety through the appropriate emergency, medical, safeguarding, legal, or protective channel.
The Cassandra process should never delay urgent protection.
Level 8 — Formal Finding
Threshold: Strong evidence reviewed through an appropriate process.
Formal findings should:
Distinguish established facts from interpretations.
Consider contrary evidence.
Be proportionate to available evidence.
Follow relevant procedural safeguards.
Level 9 — Irreversible or Public Consequence
Threshold: High evidentiary confidence appropriate to the seriousness of the consequence.
Irreversible actions require particular caution because correction may not restore:
Reputation.
Employment.
Relationships.
Privacy.
Public standing.
Immediate public safety needs may create exceptions.
3. Risk Can Change the Threshold
Low certainty does not always mean low action.
Temporary protection may be justified when:
Probability is uncertain + potential harm is severe + vulnerable people are involved + protection is reversible.
For example:
A temporary supervision change may be justified earlier than a permanent finding.
4. Reversibility Principle
Ask:
Can we reduce risk today without unnecessarily locking the system into a permanent conclusion?
Prefer reversible protections when uncertainty remains substantial.
5. Protection Is Not Punishment
Protective action should not be designed to:
Humiliate.
Retaliate.
Force confession.
Publicly shame.
Create unnecessary suffering.
Protection exists to reduce exposure to credible risk.
6. Threshold Check
Before acting, ask:
What evidence supports the concern?
What remains unknown?
How severe could the harm be?
Who is vulnerable?
What happens if we wait?
What happens if we are wrong?
Is the proposed response reversible?
Are we protecting or punishing?
HCOS™ Protection Principle
The stronger and more irreversible the consequence, the stronger the evidence generally should be.
The more severe and irreversible the possible harm, the less reasonable it may be to wait for certainty before taking proportionate protective action.

# HCOS™ Fear-to-Wisdom Stress Test Guide

**Version:** 1.5 Draft  
**Purpose:** Evaluate whether the Fear-to-Wisdom architecture remains human-centered under difficult, ambiguous, high-power, or high-risk conditions.

## How to Use This Guide

For each scenario, examine:

1. What signal triggered concern?
2. What fear entered the system?
3. What legitimate human need exists?
4. Which humans are affected?
5. What dignity requirements apply?
6. Whose autonomy is involved?
7. What perspectives are present or missing?
8. What is known, unknown, assumed, or disputed?
9. Who has decision authority, and why?
10. What power differences exist?
11. What capacity limitations are real?
12. What support could increase capacity?
13. What actions are available?
14. What is the least restrictive workable action?
15. What happens if the person refuses?
16. Can the action actually connect to access and opportunity?
17. Who bears the burden, benefit, risk, and cost?
18. What would require reassessment?
19. How would autonomy be restored?
20. What learning, repair, or transformation is needed?

---

## Stress Test 1 — Child and Family Decision

A child strongly resists a family decision that adults believe is necessary.

Test whether the framework can preserve all of the following simultaneously:

- child dignity,
- developmentally appropriate voice,
- adult responsibility,
- protection from serious harm,
- protection from adult emotional burden,
- meaningful explanation,
- and restoration of autonomy when possible.

Key question:

> **Can the child be protected from adult responsibility without being excluded from human participation?**

---

## Stress Test 2 — Healthcare Consent

A patient technically agrees to a recommendation but depends heavily on the institution controlling access to care.

Examine whether the agreement reflects meaningful consent or constrained acquiescence.

Key questions:

- Was information understandable?
- Were alternatives viable?
- Could the patient safely disagree?
- What would happen if the patient said no?
- Did dependency materially constrain choice?

---

## Stress Test 3 — Workplace Authority

An employee is told a major organizational change is optional, but refusal could harm scheduling, advancement, workload, or job security.

Examine:

- formal choice versus functional autonomy,
- power asymmetry,
- retaliation risk,
- meaningful refusal,
- organizational authority,
- and whether participation can influence implementation.

Key question:

> **Is the system offering a real choice or merely preserving the appearance of choice?**

---

## Stress Test 4 — Emergency Safety

A person faces an acute situation in which immediate action may be necessary before complete consent or deliberation is possible.

Test whether urgency is being used narrowly or becoming a general justification for expanded control.

Required sequence:

**Act → Stabilize → Explain → Listen → Reassess → Repair if Necessary → Restore Autonomy**

---

## Stress Test 5 — Disability and Supported Decision-Making

A person has reduced capacity in one domain but retains meaningful capacity in others.

Test whether the system unnecessarily generalizes one limitation into global incapacity.

Key distinction:

**Need for Support ≠ Global Incapacity**

Ask:

> **What can this person decide with appropriate support?**

---

## Stress Test 6 — Institutional Gatekeeping

A person has acted wisely and pursued available pathways but remains unable to access a needed resource because of administrative, financial, technological, logistical, or institutional barriers.

Test whether the framework wrongly attributes the failed outcome to the person.

Key distinction:

**Wise Action + No Outcome ≠ Personal Failure**

Locate the blockage:

**Connection → Access → Opportunity → Conversion**

---

## Stress Test 7 — Conflicting Autonomy

Two people legitimately want incompatible outcomes.

Test whether the framework avoids falsely assuming that human-centeredness requires agreement.

Possible human-centered outcomes may include:

- boundary,
- accommodation,
- separation,
- shared authority,
- independent authority,
- third-party stewardship,
- or respectful disagreement.

---

## Stress Test 8 — Fear-Based Organizational Response

A serious incident leads leaders to impose broad restrictions intended to prevent recurrence.

Months later, the original conditions have changed, but the restrictions remain.

Examine whether:

- evidence is being reassessed,
- the restriction remains necessary,
- authority has expanded beyond its original scope,
- convenience has replaced justification,
- and autonomy can now be restored.

Key question:

> **Has yesterday's protection become today's control?**

---

## Stress Test 9 — Hope, Spirituality, and Persistent Suffering

A person remains hopeful, prays, seeks support, takes reasonable action, and persists, but the desired outcome does not occur.

Test whether the system preserves hope without assigning responsibility for events outside the person's meaningful control.

Key distinction:

**Hope ≠ Control Over Outcome**

---

## Stress Test 10 — AI and Human Decision Support

An AI system recommends an action that a human authority could easily adopt without further review.

Examine:

- who possesses decision authority,
- whether the affected human can challenge the recommendation,
- whether the AI narrows perspective,
- whether human judgment has been displaced,
- whether explanations are meaningful,
- and where accountability resides.

Key question:

> **Is technology increasing human understanding and capacity—or merely making control easier to scale?**

---

## Pass Conditions

A stress-tested application should demonstrate that the framework can:

- distinguish protection from control,
- preserve dignity under unequal power,
- support meaningful autonomy rather than formal choice alone,
- locate authority and accountability accurately,
- recognize capacity without erasing personhood,
- preserve child voice without transferring adult responsibility,
- identify coercion and dependency pressure,
- distinguish failed pathways from personal failure,
- require reassessment of restrictions,
- restore autonomy when possible,
- and learn from outcomes rather than defend prior decisions automatically.

## Failure Signals

Revisit the framework if its application would allow any of the following without challenge:

- fear to substitute for evidence,
- authority to expand without boundaries,
- compliance to be treated as consent,
- silence to be treated as agreement,
- dependency to justify permanent control,
- one capacity limitation to erase all autonomy,
- a child to be ignored because they lack final authority,
- a child to carry adult responsibility,
- a theoretical option to be treated as meaningful access,
- a failed outcome to be attributed automatically to the person,
- or temporary restrictions to continue without reassessment.

# S-002 — HCOS™ Information Stewardship

## Human-Centered Stewardship of Information, Privacy, Provenance, Context, Access, Movement, and Use

**Status:** Foundational Stewardship Specification
**Domain:** HCOS™ Stewardship
**Identifier:** S-002
**Parent Specification:** S-000 — HCOS™ Stewardship
**Related Specification:** S-001 — HCOS™ Evidence Stewardship
**Applies To:** HCOS™ frameworks, instruments, assessments, AI systems, organizations, healthcare systems, families, institutions, governance systems, knowledge systems, decision processes, and human-centered analysis

---

# 1. Purpose

HCOS™ Information Stewardship defines how human-centered systems responsibly create, receive, collect, preserve, contextualize, access, transform, share, restrict, correct, use, and retire information.

Information creates power.

It can help people:

* understand;
* coordinate;
* protect;
* learn;
* make decisions;
* receive care;
* establish accountability;
* preserve memory;
* and prevent future harm.

Information can also create harm.

It can:

* expose;
* misrepresent;
* shame;
* manipulate;
* overwhelm;
* exclude;
* stigmatize;
* coerce;
* surveil;
* discredit;
* or permanently alter how another person is perceived.

Information therefore should not be treated merely as something a person or system possesses.

> **Information creates stewardship responsibilities.**

The purpose of HCOS™ Information Stewardship is to preserve both the usefulness of information and the dignity, autonomy, privacy, context, and safety of the humans connected to it.

---

# 2. Core Information Stewardship Principle

> **Access to information does not automatically create a right to use, share, interpret, retain, or expose it without limitation.**

Healthy systems ask:

* Where did this information come from?
* Why was it collected?
* Is it accurate?
* What context belongs with it?
* Who needs access?
* Who does not?
* Was consent given where appropriate?
* What could happen if it moves?
* What could happen if it does not move?
* What transformations has it undergone?
* How should it be used?
* How long should it remain?
* Can the person connected to the information understand or challenge it?
* What responsibilities arise from possessing it?

---

# 3. Information Is Relational

Information rarely exists in isolation.

It has relationships to:

* a source;
* a person;
* an event;
* a purpose;
* a context;
* a time;
* a recipient;
* a decision;
* a system;
* and possible future uses.

For example, a statement may be accurate but become misleading when separated from:

* when it was made;
* why it was made;
* what happened immediately before;
* what happened afterward;
* whether the circumstances later changed;
* or whether the person later corrected it.

Therefore:

> **Information Stewardship protects not only information, but the relationships necessary to understand it responsibly.**

---

# 4. Information Is Not Evidence by Default

Information and evidence overlap, but they are not identical.

Information may include:

* observations;
* opinions;
* interpretations;
* rumors;
* records;
* predictions;
* memories;
* summaries;
* measurements;
* AI outputs;
* hypotheses;
* personal disclosures;
* or administrative data.

Whether information functions as evidence depends upon the question being considered and the quality of the information.

S-001 Evidence Stewardship asks:

> **What does this information support?**

S-002 Information Stewardship asks:

> **How should this information be cared for?**

Both questions matter.

---

# 5. The Information Lifecycle

HCOS treats information as moving through a lifecycle.

## 1. Creation

Information comes into existence.

## 2. Collection

Someone or some system acquires it.

## 3. Classification

Its type, sensitivity, purpose, and importance are identified.

## 4. Contextualization

Relevant meaning, source, time, limitations, and relationships are preserved.

## 5. Storage

The information is retained somewhere.

## 6. Access

People or systems are permitted to retrieve it.

## 7. Movement

Information is communicated, transferred, copied, summarized, or disclosed.

## 8. Transformation

Information may be:

* summarized;
* translated;
* aggregated;
* analyzed;
* inferred from;
* reformatted;
* compressed;
* scored;
* categorized;
* or interpreted.

## 9. Use

Information influences decisions, actions, relationships, policies, or understanding.

## 10. Correction

Errors, omissions, outdated information, or misleading interpretations are addressed.

## 11. Preservation

Information with continuing legitimate value is maintained.

## 12. Retirement

Information that no longer has sufficient purpose may be archived, restricted, deleted, or otherwise removed from active use where appropriate.

Stewardship responsibilities exist throughout the lifecycle.

# 6. Purpose Stewardship

Information should be connected to a legitimate purpose.

Ask:

* Why are we collecting this?
* Why are we keeping it?
* Why are we sharing it?
* Why are we analyzing it?
* Why does this person or system need access?
* Has the purpose changed?
* Is the new use compatible with the original reason the information was provided?

A system should be cautious when information collected for one purpose becomes useful for another.

Something disclosed for:

* care;
* support;
* safety;
* employment;
* education;
* legal guidance;
* or personal trust

should not automatically become available for unrelated purposes.

> **Availability does not erase purpose.**

---

# 7. Privacy Stewardship

Privacy is not merely secrecy.

Privacy includes appropriate human control over:

* personal information;
* identity;
* health;
* relationships;
* communication;
* location;
* finances;
* experiences;
* vulnerabilities;
* and personal narrative.

Privacy Stewardship asks:

* Is this information sensitive?
* Does this person reasonably expect privacy?
* Who has a legitimate role?
* Is disclosure necessary?
* Could less information accomplish the same purpose?
* Could identifying details be removed?
* What harm could disclosure create?
* Does the person understand how the information may be used?

### Critical Principle

> **Knowing something about another person does not make that information ours to distribute.**

---

# 8. Consent Stewardship

Consent matters when information is collected, accessed, or shared.

Healthy consent should be:

* meaningful;
* understandable;
* appropriately informed;
* sufficiently voluntary;
* specific enough to matter;
* and revisable where circumstances allow.

Ask:

* Did the person know information would be collected?
* Did they understand why?
* Did they know who might receive it?
* Were they realistically able to decline?
* Was consent obtained under pressure?
* Has the use expanded beyond what they reasonably understood?
* Can consent be withdrawn where appropriate?

Consent is not always the only legitimate basis for information use.

Safety, legal obligations, public responsibilities, care, or other legitimate needs may sometimes justify limited use without explicit consent.

When that occurs, stewardship becomes even more important.

Use only what is necessary.

Share only with those who need it.

Preserve dignity.

Document why the exception was justified.

---

# 9. Minimum Necessary Information

More information is not always better.

Collecting or sharing excessive information can increase:

* privacy risk;
* bias;
* misunderstanding;
* surveillance;
* cognitive load;
* security exposure;
* and future misuse.

Ask:

> **What is the minimum information necessary to accomplish the legitimate purpose?**

This principle applies to:

* data collection;
* family communication;
* healthcare;
* workplaces;
* AI systems;
* legal processes;
* education;
* research;
* and institutional governance.

---

# 10. Context Stewardship

Information without context can become misinformation even when the underlying facts are technically accurate.

Context may include:

* date;
* setting;
* circumstances;
* source;
* purpose;
* preceding events;
* subsequent events;
* uncertainty;
* limitations;
* developmental factors;
* cultural factors;
* and whether conditions have changed.

Example:

> “The person was unable to complete the task.”

may mean something very different from:

> “The person was unable to complete the task after working without adequate recovery during an acute family crisis.”

The first statement may be factually accurate.

The second may be substantially more truthful.

> **Accuracy without sufficient context can still produce distortion.**

---

# 11. Provenance Stewardship

Important information should remain connected to its origin.

Ask:

* Who originally generated this information?
* Is this the original version?
* Has it been summarized?
* Who summarized it?
* Has interpretation been added?
* Has AI transformed it?
* Has it passed through multiple people or systems?
* Can the original still be accessed where appropriate?

A provenance chain might look like:

**Person → Original Statement → Record → Summary → AI Analysis → Decision**

Each transformation should remain distinguishable.

This prevents derived information from silently becoming indistinguishable from source information.

---

# 12. Information Movement

Information changes systems when it moves.

Movement may occur through:

* conversation;
* email;
* text;
* social media;
* records;
* databases;
* meetings;
* reports;
* family networks;
* professional networks;
* APIs;
* AI systems;
* or informal communication.

Ask:

* Who initiated the movement?
* Who received the information?
* Why?
* Was the recipient necessary?
* What changed after they received it?
* Was additional information added?
* Did meaning change?
* Did the information generate support, understanding, pressure, punishment, or exclusion?

Information movement itself can reveal important system conditions.

---

# 13. Information Network Effects

One disclosure can produce effects far beyond the original exchange.

For example:

**Person A shares information with Person B**

↓

**Person B interprets it**

↓

**Person B tells Persons C and D**

↓

**C and D contact Person A**

↓

**Person A experiences social pressure**

The original disclosure has now become a network event.

HCOS therefore asks not only:

> “Was the information accurate?”

but also:

> **“What did the movement of this information cause within the human system?”**

This connects Information Stewardship directly with S-004 Connection Stewardship.

---

# 14. Triangulation

Triangulation may occur when information is moved through a third party rather than communication occurring appropriately between the people directly involved.

Triangulation is not always harmful.

A mediator, advocate, clinician, attorney, trusted support person, or safeguarding professional may appropriately serve as a third party.

The question is:

> **What function is the third party serving?**

Healthy third-party involvement may:

* increase safety;
* improve understanding;
* provide expertise;
* preserve boundaries;
* facilitate communication.

Unhealthy triangulation may:

* increase pressure;
* avoid accountability;
* distort information;
* recruit allies;
* undermine direct communication;
* or isolate one person.

Evaluate function rather than applying the label automatically.

---

# 15. Coalition-Building

Information may be used to recruit people into a conflict.

Ask:

* Were additional people needed?
* Were they given balanced information?
* Were they asked to help understand or to take sides?
* Did their involvement increase safety?
* Did it increase pressure?
* Did one person become socially surrounded?
* Did the coalition acquire power over the person's choices?

Coalition-building can transform a disagreement between two people into a system-wide power imbalance.

---

# 16. Narrative Control

Information can shape who is allowed to define reality.

Narrative control may occur when:

* one person's interpretation circulates before another can speak;
* selective facts are shared;
* relevant context is withheld;
* private information is used to establish credibility or discredit another person;
* one authority becomes the accepted narrator;
* corrections do not travel as widely as the original claim.

Ask:

> **Who currently controls the story?**

Then ask:

> **Does control of the story reflect evidentiary strength, or merely greater access to the information network?**

---

# 17. Credibility Undermining

Personal information should not be used merely to weaken a person's ability to raise a concern.

Examples may include unnecessary references to:

* health history;
* emotional reactions;
* previous mistakes;
* financial circumstances;
* relationship history;
* diagnoses;
* treatment;
* or unrelated personal information.

Such information may sometimes legitimately matter.

The question is relevance.

Ask:

> **Does this information help evaluate the concern, or is it being used to make the person easier to dismiss?**

---

# 18. Information and Power

Information is a form of power.

People with access to information may be able to:

* define narratives;
* influence decisions;
* anticipate actions;
* expose vulnerabilities;
* control opportunities;
* affect reputation;
* or determine who receives support.

Power Stewardship therefore asks:

* Who has access?
* Who does not?
* Who can correct information?
* Who can see what has been said about them?
* Who can distribute information?
* Who can prevent distribution?
* Who benefits from informational asymmetry?
* Who bears the consequences?

> **Greater informational power creates greater stewardship responsibility.**

---

# 19. Information and Perspective

Different people may possess different information.

Their perspectives may therefore differ for understandable reasons.

Person A may know X.

Person B may know Y.

Person C may know X and Y but not Z.

An apparent disagreement may partly reflect unequal information access.

Perspective Stewardship therefore asks:

> **What information is available from each position?**

Information Stewardship asks:

> **Should additional information appropriately be shared to improve understanding?**

Sometimes greater transparency helps.

Sometimes privacy requires limits.

Stewardship balances both.

---

# 20. Information and Capacity

Information itself creates human load.

Too little information can create uncertainty.

Too much information can create overwhelm.

Poorly organized information can create cognitive fragmentation.

Repeated requests for the same information can create unnecessary burden.

Complex systems may transfer the work of integration onto the human.

Information Stewardship therefore asks:

* How much information can this person reasonably process?
* Is the information understandable?
* Is it organized?
* Is the important information distinguishable from noise?
* Are people repeatedly asked to reconstruct information the system already possesses?
* Could the system reduce unnecessary information burden?

> **Information should increase human capacity when possible, not consume it unnecessarily.**

---

# 21. Information Transformation

Information frequently changes form.

Examples include:

* transcription;
* summarization;
* translation;
* aggregation;
* scoring;
* classification;
* visualization;
* inference;
* AI analysis;
* and decision support.

Transformation may add enormous value.

It may also introduce distortion.

Whenever information is transformed, ask:

* What was preserved?
* What was removed?
* What interpretation was added?
* What uncertainty disappeared?
* Can the transformation be traced back to its source?
* Could a reasonable person distinguish the original information from the derived interpretation?

---

# 22. Correction Stewardship

Incorrect information should be correctable.

Healthy systems create pathways for:

* correction;
* clarification;
* contextualization;
* dispute;
* updating;
* and supersession.

Ask:

* Can the affected person identify an error?
* Can they challenge it?
* Who reviews corrections?
* Does the correction remain connected to the original record?
* Does corrected information propagate to downstream systems?
* Will future decisions rely on the corrected version?

A system that permits information to spread but makes correction difficult creates asymmetric harm.

---

# 23. Information Supersession

Information may once have been accurate and later become outdated.

Examples:

* old diagnoses;
* prior employment status;
* previous risk assessments;
* outdated contact information;
* earlier hypotheses;
* former policies;
* superseded clinical recommendations;
* resolved concerns.

Healthy systems distinguish:

**historically accurate**

from

**currently applicable**.

Old information should not silently govern current decisions merely because it remains available.

---

# 24. Information Preservation

Some information should be preserved because it supports:

* accountability;
* continuity;
* learning;
* legal obligations;
* historical understanding;
* patient care;
* system memory;
* or future evidence.

Preservation should maintain:

* integrity;
* provenance;
* context;
* access controls;
* and appropriate privacy.

Preservation is not the same as unrestricted availability.

---

# 25. Information Retirement

Keeping everything forever is not necessarily good stewardship.

Information may eventually:

* lose relevance;
* create unnecessary privacy risk;
* increase system clutter;
* perpetuate outdated narratives;
* or exceed legitimate retention needs.

Ask:

* Is there still a legitimate purpose for retaining this?
* Is retention required?
* Could it be archived rather than actively surfaced?
* Should access become more restricted?
* Could deletion be appropriate?
* What would be lost if the information disappeared?

Future Stewardship should inform retention decisions.

---

# 26. Information Stewardship and Children

Information about children requires particular care.

Children may have limited control over:

* what adults record;
* what adults share;
* how adults describe them;
* what institutions retain;
* and what narratives follow them.

Ask:

* Does this information need to be shared?
* Is the description developmentally fair?
* Could a temporary behavior become a permanent identity label?
* Is adult conflict being documented as though it belongs to the child?
* Could the record unnecessarily shape how future adults perceive the child?
* Is the child's dignity being preserved?

> **Children should not inherit permanent informational identities created from temporary periods of difficulty.**

---

# 27. Information Stewardship and AI

AI changes information stewardship because AI can:

* retrieve;
* combine;
* summarize;
* infer;
* classify;
* connect;
* generate;
* remember;
* transform;
* and redistribute information at extraordinary scale.

This creates both opportunity and responsibility.

HCOS-aligned AI should distinguish among:

### Source Information

What came from the original human, record, system, measurement, or source.

### Derived Information

What has been calculated, summarized, aggregated, or transformed.

### Inference

What the AI believes may reasonably follow.

### Hypothesis

A possible explanation generated from available information.

### Recommendation

An action suggested by the AI.

These categories should not silently collapse into one another.

---

# 28. AI Information Laundering

A particular risk occurs when AI-generated interpretation later appears to be independent factual information.

Example:

**Original Source**

↓

**AI Summary**

↓

**AI Interpretation**

↓

**Summary copied into another system**

↓

**Later AI treats the copied interpretation as source evidence**

The interpretation may eventually appear independently corroborated even though all versions originated from the same inference.

HCOS calls this **AI Information Laundering**.

Healthy AI systems should preserve provenance sufficiently to reduce this risk.

---

# 29. AI Memory Stewardship

AI systems that maintain memory create additional responsibilities.

Ask:

* Why is this information being remembered?
* Is it still accurate?
* Is it still relevant?
* Is it sensitive?
* Does the person expect it to persist?
* Has newer information superseded it?
* Is an interpretation being stored as though it were a fact?
* Could memory create unfair future assumptions?
* Can inappropriate information be corrected or forgotten?

AI memory should not become an invisible permanent narrative about a human being.

---

# 30. Information Stewardship Failure Patterns

## Provenance Loss

Information becomes detached from its origin.

## Context Collapse

Accurate information becomes misleading because relevant context disappears.

## Privacy Leakage

Sensitive information moves beyond legitimate recipients.

## Purpose Drift

Information collected for one reason is used for unrelated purposes without sufficient justification.

## Overcollection

Systems gather more information than they reasonably need.

## Overexposure

More information is shared than necessary.

## Narrative Capture

One person or institution controls the accepted account.

## Coalition Amplification

Information recruits additional people into a conflict and increases pressure.

## Correction Asymmetry

Incorrect information spreads easily while corrections do not.

## Historical Lock-In

Outdated information continues controlling present decisions.

## Capacity Overload

Information volume or complexity exceeds reasonable human processing capacity.

## Transformation Loss

Summarization or analysis removes essential meaning or uncertainty.

## AI Information Laundering

AI-generated interpretation reappears as apparent source evidence.

## Informational Power Concentration

One party controls information, access, correction, and interpretation without meaningful accountability.

# 31. The HCOS™ Information Stewardship Check

Before collecting, sharing, transforming, or using important information, ask:

1. What is the information?
2. Where did it originate?
3. Why do we have it?
4. Is it accurate?
5. What context belongs with it?
6. Is it sensitive?
7. Who is connected to it?
8. Was consent obtained where appropriate?
9. Who legitimately needs access?
10. What is the minimum necessary information?
11. What happens if it is shared?
12. What happens if it is not shared?
13. Has it been transformed?
14. Can the transformation be distinguished from the source?
15. Could the person correct or contextualize it?
16. Has newer information superseded it?
17. How does information movement affect power?
18. Does it create unnecessary human load?
19. Should it be preserved?
20. Should access be limited?
21. Should it eventually be retired?
22. What future consequences could arise from retaining or sharing it?
23. Are we using information to understand and protect—or to shame, pressure, control, or dominate?

# 32. HCOS™ Information Stewardship Standard

A human-centered system should be able to demonstrate that it:

* identifies legitimate information purposes;
* preserves meaningful provenance;
* protects context;
* respects privacy;
* uses consent appropriately;
* limits collection and disclosure where possible;
* manages access proportionately;
* distinguishes source information from transformation and inference;
* tracks important information movement;
* considers power effects;
* provides reasonable correction pathways;
* recognizes superseded information;
* preserves necessary records responsibly;
* retires information when appropriate;
* protects children and vulnerable people from unnecessary informational harm;
* considers human cognitive capacity;
* prevents information from becoming an instrument of unnecessary coercion;
* and maintains accountability for AI-mediated information transformation.

# 33. Information Stewardship Principle

> **Information is not something to win with.**
>
> **It is something to steward.**

Possessing information creates responsibility.

Sharing information creates consequences.

Transforming information creates interpretive responsibility.

Preserving information creates future responsibility.

Restricting information creates power.

Deleting information creates loss.

Every stage requires judgment.

Healthy systems therefore ask not merely:

> “Can we use this information?”

They ask:

> **“What is the most responsible way to care for this information and the humans connected to it?”**

---

# 34. Closing Principle

Information can illuminate reality.

It can also reshape reality.

It can protect someone.

It can expose someone.

It can preserve truth.

It can preserve an error.

It can connect people.

It can mobilize people against one another.

It can increase autonomy.

It can create control.

The difference is not information alone.

The difference is stewardship.

HCOS™ Information Stewardship therefore protects:

**source**

↓

**context**

↓

**privacy**

↓

**purpose**

↓

**access**

↓

**movement**

↓

**transformation**

↓

**use**

↓

**correction**

↓

**preservation**

↓

**learning**

↓

**future consequence**

and then asks again:

> **Does this information still serve the humans and legitimate purposes for which it is being stewarded?**

Healthy systems do not treat information as neutral simply because it is available.

They recognize that information has relationships, consequences, power, and memory.

And they accept responsibility for all four.

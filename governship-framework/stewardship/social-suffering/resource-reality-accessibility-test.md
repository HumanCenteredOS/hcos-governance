# HCOS™ Resource Reality & Accessibility Test

## Human-Centered Evaluation of Whether Help Is Actually Reachable, Usable, and Timely

**Framework:** Human-Centered Operating Systems™ (HCOS™)
**Domain:** Social Suffering & Human Vulnerability Stewardship
**Type:** Resource Evaluation, Navigation, and Stewardship Instrument
**Version:** 1.0 Draft

---

## 1. Purpose

Systems frequently respond to human need by identifying resources.

A resource may technically exist.

A website may list it.

A database may return it.

An AI system may recommend it.

A social worker may refer someone to it.

A platform may display a link.

But none of these establish that meaningful help is actually available to the human.

A resource can exist while remaining inaccessible because of:

* eligibility
* cost
* timing
* waitlists
* geography
* transportation
* documentation
* disability
* childcare
* language
* technology
* administrative complexity
* safety
* social circumstances
* depleted human capacity

The HCOS™ Resource Reality & Accessibility Test asks:

## Can this human actually use this resource under the conditions they are living in right now?

---

# 2. Core Distinction

HCOS separates four different states:

## Resource Existence

The resource exists somewhere.

↓

## Resource Availability

The resource is currently operating and accepting eligible people.

↓

## Resource Accessibility

The specific person can realistically reach and use it.

↓

## Resource Effectiveness

The resource actually reduces the identified burden.

These conditions should not be treated as interchangeable.

---

# 3. Core Principle

## A resource is not meaningfully available until it is usable by the human who needs it.

This means that:

**Resource identified**

does not equal

**Resource accessible**

and:

**Resource accessed**

does not necessarily equal

**Need resolved**

---

# 4. Why Resource Reality Matters

Systems often measure successful support through:

* referral made
* information delivered
* link clicked
* application started
* phone number provided
* appointment recommended

These measures primarily describe what the system did.

HCOS asks instead:

## What happened to the human?

Did the person:

* qualify?
* reach someone?
* obtain the service?
* receive help soon enough?
* overcome the barrier?
* experience reduced burden?

The distinction prevents false success.

---

# 5. The Resource Reality Pathway

Use:

## Need

↓

## Candidate Resource

↓

## Availability

↓

## Eligibility

↓

## Accessibility

↓

## Timing

↓

## Burden of Access

↓

## Safety and Trust

↓

## Human Choice

↓

## Actual Connection

↓

## Capacity Return

At any point, a resource may fail.

When it does, the underlying need remains active.

---

# 6. Step One — Define the Need

Before evaluating resources, identify what the human actually needs.

Examples:

* transportation
* housing
* food
* medication
* childcare
* financial assistance
* legal support
* emotional support
* caregiving help
* insurance navigation
* safe shelter
* employment support
* human connection

Avoid beginning with the resource category.

Begin with:

## What outcome is necessary for the human?

---

# 7. Step Two — Confirm Resource Existence

Ask:

* Does the resource still exist?
* Is the organization operating?
* Is the information current?
* Is the program offered in this location?
* Is the service relevant to the identified need?

Outdated directories can create false pathways.

A resource that closed six months ago should not remain part of an active support plan.

---

# 8. Step Three — Test Current Availability

A resource may exist but lack capacity.

Ask:

* Is it accepting applications?
* Are appointments available?
* Are beds available?
* Is funding currently available?
* Is the program full?
* Is there a waiting list?
* Has the annual allocation been exhausted?
* Is the service available today?

Example:

**Resource:** After-school childcare program

**Status:** Program exists

**Reality:** Enrollment is full

HCOS conclusion:

## Resource exists, but is not currently available.

---

# 9. Step Four — Test Eligibility

Ask whether the specific human qualifies.

Possible restrictions include:

* income
* age
* diagnosis
* geographic location
* insurance
* employment status
* immigration status
* household composition
* documentation
* previous participation

Do not ask the person to complete a burdensome application when basic eligibility can be determined first.

---

# 10. Step Five — Test Timing

Help must arrive before the relevant consequence.

Example:

**Need:** Prevent eviction Friday

**Program:** Emergency rental assistance

**Processing time:** Four weeks

The program may be useful later.

But it is not an adequate immediate bridge.

HCOS asks:

## Is the resource timely enough to alter the consequence?

---

# 11. Step Six — Test Financial Accessibility

A supposedly free or subsidized resource may still require:

* application fees
* transportation
* parking
* deposits
* copays
* phone service
* internet
* unpaid time away from work
* childcare
* upfront purchase followed by reimbursement

Ask:

## What does it actually cost the person to use this resource?

Cost should include more than the stated program fee.

---

# 12. Step Seven — Test Geographic Accessibility

Ask:

* How far away is the service?
* Is transportation available?
* Is public transportation realistic?
* Does weather affect access?
* Does the person have a safe vehicle?
* Can the person travel with children?
* Is the resource usable in a rural area?

A theoretically available service two hours away may not be meaningfully accessible.

---

# 13. Step Eight — Test Transportation Reality

A system should not assume:

**The person can get there.**

Possible barriers include:

* no vehicle
* unreliable vehicle
* no driver's license
* fuel cost
* lack of public transportation
* disability
* need to transport children
* work schedule

If transportation is the barrier, transportation itself becomes part of the intervention.

---

# 14. Step Nine — Test Childcare Reality

Many resources unintentionally assume adults can attend:

* appointments
* court
* interviews
* counseling
* benefits offices
* medical visits
* training

without children.

Ask:

* Are children allowed?
* Is childcare available?
* Who will supervise them?
* What does childcare cost?
* Does the timing overlap with school?

If the person cannot safely leave their children, an otherwise accessible resource may not be usable.

---

# 15. Step Ten — Test Administrative Accessibility

Ask what the person must do to access help.

Requirements may include:

* multiple forms
* income verification
* identification
* tax returns
* medical records
* employer documents
* phone interviews
* in-person appointments
* repeated explanations
* signatures
* appeals

HCOS asks:

## How much human capacity does obtaining this resource consume?

A program intended to reduce suffering should not require unreasonable suffering to access it.

---

# 16. Administrative Burden Ratio

HCOS introduces the concept:

## Administrative Burden Ratio

Compare:

**Burden required to obtain help**

with:

**Benefit expected from the help**

A resource becomes increasingly problematic when accessing it requires disproportionate:

* time
* cognitive effort
* travel
* documentation
* emotional exposure
* financial cost

For highly vulnerable humans, acceptable administrative burden should generally decrease.

---

# 17. Step Eleven — Test Cognitive Accessibility

Information may be technically available but difficult to understand.

Ask:

* Are instructions clear?
* Is specialized knowledge required?
* Are choices understandable?
* Does the person have to compare many programs?
* Is information contradictory?
* Can AI or a navigator reduce complexity?

HCOS principle:

## Complexity should decrease as human load increases.

---

# 18. Step Twelve — Test Disability Accessibility

Ask whether the resource supports:

* mobility needs
* sensory needs
* cognitive disability
* communication differences
* visual impairment
* hearing impairment
* neurodivergence
* chronic illness

Accessibility cannot be assumed because a program technically complies with minimum requirements.

The relevant question is:

## Can this particular human use the service successfully?

---

# 19. Step Thirteen — Test Language and Communication Access

Ask:

* Is interpretation available?
* Is written information understandable?
* Can the person communicate in their preferred language?
* Does the service require phone communication?
* Can text or email be used?
* Are accommodations available?

Communication barriers can transform a resource into an inaccessible one.

---

# 20. Step Fourteen — Test Technology Access

Digital resource systems may assume access to:

* smartphone
* computer
* internet
* email
* printer
* scanner
* electronic signatures
* online portals
* digital identity verification

Ask:

## Does the person actually have the technology required to complete the process?

If not, the system should provide another pathway.

---

# 21. Step Fifteen — Test Safety

Some resources may be technically accessible but unsafe.

Examples:

* shelter location visible to an abusive partner
* family assistance requiring contact with unsafe relatives
* transportation from an unknown individual
* public fundraising exposing a child's medical information
* community support requiring disclosure of a home address

Ask:

## Could accessing this resource increase risk?

Safety should be part of resource evaluation, not an afterthought.

---

# 22. Step Sixteen — Test Trustworthiness

People experiencing crisis may be particularly vulnerable to:

* scams
* misinformation
* impersonation
* exploitative services
* predatory financial products
* fraudulent charities
* dangerous medical claims

Ask:

* Is the organization legitimate?
* Is information current?
* Are fees transparent?
* Is sensitive data protected?
* Are there credible accountability mechanisms?

HCOS favors:

## Verified help over merely available help.

---

# 23. Step Seventeen — Test Social Support Assumptions

Some resources require informal support.

Examples:

**“Bring someone to drive you home.”**

**“Have a family member watch the children.”**

**“Stay with friends temporarily.”**

**“Ask someone to help complete the forms.”**

Ask:

## Does this person actually have that support?

If not, the assumed support becomes another missing resource.

---

# 24. Step Eighteen — Test Emotional Cost

Seeking help can require repeated disclosure of:

* trauma
* poverty
* illness
* abuse
* disability
* family circumstances

Repeated retelling can create significant emotional burden.

Ask:

* How often must the person explain their situation?
* Can information be shared with consent across services?
* Is unnecessary disclosure required?
* Is dignity preserved?

Help should not require repeated humiliation.

---

# 25. Step Nineteen — Test Human Choice

A resource should not automatically be considered appropriate simply because it is accessible.

Ask:

## Does the person actually want this form of help?

Consider:

* privacy
* cultural preferences
* family needs
* personal values
* prior experiences
* safety
* autonomy

An intervention rejected for meaningful reasons should not be counted as successful simply because the system offered it.

---

# 26. Step Twenty — Test Actual Connection

Determine whether the handoff succeeded.

Examples:

**Referral given**

is different from:

**appointment scheduled**

which is different from:

**service received**

which is different from:

**burden reduced**

HCOS should track the meaningful point of connection whenever possible and appropriate.

---

# 27. Resource Status Categories

HCOS recommends classifying candidate resources as:

## A — Immediately Usable

Available, eligible, accessible, timely, safe, and acceptable.

## B — Usable With a Bridge

Resource can work if another barrier is solved.

Example:

Program is available but transportation is missing.

## C — Future Option

Potentially helpful but not soon enough for the current need.

## D — Technically Existing but Inaccessible

Resource exists but cannot realistically be used by this person.

## E — Unsafe or Inappropriate

Resource creates unacceptable risk, burden, or conflict with human choice.

## F — Unknown

Insufficient information exists to determine usability.

This prevents every search result from being presented as an equivalent option.

---

# 28. Bridge Identification

When a resource falls into:

## B — Usable With a Bridge

identify the missing bridge.

Examples:

### Resource Available

Medical appointment.

### Barrier

No transportation.

### Bridge

Ride or transportation voucher.

---

### Resource Available

Rental assistance.

### Barrier

Person cannot gather documentation.

### Bridge

Human navigator assists with application.

---

### Resource Available

Support group.

### Barrier

No childcare.

### Bridge

Childcare support or remote participation.

---

# 29. When No Usable Resource Exists

Sometimes the correct conclusion is:

## No currently identified resource adequately meets this need.

HCOS should not disguise this reality.

Instead ask:

* Can deterioration be delayed?
* Can another burden be reduced?
* Can a temporary bridge be created?
* Who has authority to make an exception?
* Is advocacy needed?
* Does the unmet need indicate a structural capacity failure?

An honest identification of a resource gap is preferable to a false referral.

---

# 30. System Capacity Gap

If a needed resource repeatedly exists only in theory, HCOS identifies a:

## System Capacity Gap

Examples:

* childcare programs consistently full
* mental-health services with six-month waits
* housing assistance without available housing
* transportation assistance unavailable in rural areas
* legal services unable to accept new cases

The human should not be blamed for failing to access capacity that the system does not possess.

---

# 31. Resource Mirage

HCOS introduces:

## Resource Mirage

A Resource Mirage occurs when a system appears to offer help because resources can be named or displayed, but those resources are not realistically usable.

Example:

A platform tells a person:

**“Here are ten resources.”**

But:

* six are closed,
* two have long waiting lists,
* one requires eligibility the person does not meet,
* one is geographically inaccessible.

The system has produced the appearance of support without meaningful support.

---

# 32. Resource Dumping

Another failure mode is:

## Resource Dumping

Resource Dumping occurs when a person is given a large volume of resources without:

* prioritization
* accessibility testing
* relevance
* navigation
* follow-through

This transfers the work of solving the problem back to the person experiencing the crisis.

HCOS asks systems to carry more of that evaluation burden.

---

# 33. AI Resource Navigation

AI may be particularly useful for:

* identifying possible resources
* checking eligibility criteria
* comparing timelines
* organizing documentation
* prioritizing options
* identifying access barriers
* summarizing requirements
* reducing repetitive search

But AI should not merely generate more links.

The appropriate role is:

## Search → Evaluate → Prioritize → Explain → Connect

The human remains the decision-maker.

---

# 34. Human Navigation

Some resource situations require human assistance.

A navigator may be necessary when:

* several systems interact
* eligibility is uncertain
* exceptions may be needed
* advocacy is required
* the person has low remaining capacity
* the consequences are severe

Possible navigators include:

* social worker
* case manager
* patient advocate
* benefits navigator
* housing navigator
* legal advocate
* community worker
* peer navigator

---

# 35. Capacity Return Test

After a resource is accessed, ask:

## Did the resource meaningfully return capacity?

Examples:

### No Capacity Return

Resource provided but burden unchanged.

### Informational Return

Person understands options.

### Access Return

Service becomes reachable.

### Functional Return

Concrete burden decreases.

### Sustained Capacity Return

Functioning improves over time.

### Structural Capacity Return

System conditions improve for others too.

---

# 36. Example — Pediatric Cancer

### Need

Travel to cancer treatment.

### Candidate Resource

Transportation assistance organization.

### Test

**Exists:** Yes
**Accepting applications:** Yes
**Eligible:** Yes
**Processing:** 10 days
**Next treatment:** 2 days

### Conclusion

Resource is:

## C — Future Option

The immediate need remains unmet.

HCOS should continue searching for:

## an immediate transportation bridge.

---

# 37. Example — Financial Crisis

### Need

Emergency rent assistance.

### Candidate Resource

County assistance program.

### Test

**Eligible:** Likely
**Application:** Available
**Processing:** 30 days
**Eviction deadline:** 5 days

### Conclusion

The program may support longer-term stabilization but does not solve the immediate risk.

A second intervention is needed.

---

# 38. Example — Social Isolation

### Need

Backup support for childcare.

### Suggested Resource

“Ask family.”

### Resource Reality

No safe or available family support exists.

### Conclusion

This is not a resource.

The system must identify another pathway.

HCOS recognizes:

## Assumed support is not actual support.

---

# 39. Quick Resource Reality Test

For every important resource, ask:

**Need**
What outcome is required?

**Existence**
Does the resource still exist?

**Availability**
Is it currently accepting people?

**Eligibility**
Does the person qualify?

**Timing**
Will help arrive soon enough?

**Cost**
What does access actually cost?

**Geography**
Can the person reach it?

**Transportation**
Can they get there?

**Childcare**
Can they participate while caring for dependents?

**Administration**
How much paperwork or coordination is required?

**Technology**
What digital access is required?

**Disability**
Is the service genuinely accessible?

**Communication**
Can the person understand and interact with the service?

**Safety**
Could accessing it create harm?

**Trust**
Is the resource credible?

**Social Support**
Does it assume help the person does not have?

**Human Capacity**
Can the person realistically complete the process?

**Choice**
Does the person want this option?

**Outcome**
Did the resource actually reduce the need?

---

# 40. Stewardship Standard

A human-centered system should never count:

## “Resource provided”

as sufficient evidence of:

## “Human helped.”

A resource becomes meaningful only when it can cross the distance between:

**what exists**

and

**what the human can actually reach.**

---

# 41. Core HCOS Principle

The question is not:

## “Is there a resource?”

The question is:

## “Can this person actually use it, in time, under the conditions of their real life?”

If not, the work of stewardship is not complete.

## Help is not real until it is reachable.
